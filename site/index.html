About OpenFloat

Supplier finance runs on code nobody can read.

A small supplier waiting 60 days for a large buyer's money pays around 30–57% a year to get it today, priced off its own credit.1 Once the buyer confirms it will pay, the same invoice could be funded at close to the buyer's rate. That gap is captured today inside closed platforms, one operator per silo.

Open, neutral and boring by design.

  • Zero platform fees

    The supplier pays only the funder's rate.

  • Zero operators

    Every participant runs its own node.

  • One funder per invoice

    Checked by the buyer and a public log.

  • Zero custody

    Banks move money. Nodes send instructions.

  • Zero lock-in

    Apache-2.0 code. CC BY 4.0 spec.

How it works

Getting paid early shouldn't need a platform.

The old way

  1. Apply to a factorCredit check on the supplier, slow onboarding
  2. Pay 1–5% a monthPriced off the supplier's own credit
  3. Or join the buyer's platformOne operator, one silo, fees not public
  4. Nobody can check the restDouble financing found too late

With OpenFloat

  1. The buyer signs a promise to payAnyone can verify it
  2. Banks and the buyer's cash competePriced off the buyer's credit
  3. The buyer acknowledges one funderEvery other claim is rejected
  4. Money moves on existing bank railsSigned confirmations at each step

FAQs

What is OpenFloat for?

Letting a supplier with a buyer-approved invoice get paid early by whoever offers the cheapest money: the buyer's own cash, at a discount, or a bank funding against the buyer's signed promise to pay.

Who runs it?

Nobody in particular. It is a protocol: buyers, suppliers and funders each run a node and exchange signed messages. The buyer, who owes the money, is the source of truth for who gets paid. The intent is a neutral foundation for the spec, with no single commercial owner.

How does money actually move?

Through the banks participants already use. Nodes produce ISO 20022 payment files and exchange signed confirmations of what their banks report, plus overdue notices if a payment does not arrive. The protocol never holds funds.

How do you stop an invoice being financed twice?

Three checks: the buyer acknowledges one funder per invoice, the supplier signs that the invoice is not pledged elsewhere, and funders check a public, hash-chained log of acknowledgments before paying.

Is it production ready?

No. It is a draft spec (openfloat/0.1) with a reference implementation and a simulation on synthetic data. In that simulation, suppliers paid 82–85% less than with their own factoring and every double-financing attempt was blocked. Those are synthetic results, not a claim about any real programme.

Why not a blockchain?

For approved invoices there is already a party everyone can rely on: the buyer that owes the money. A shared ledger adds an operator problem without solving one. The acknowledgment log borrows only the useful part: append-only, hash-chained, verifiable by anyone.
