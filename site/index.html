An open protocol for financing at the payment layer

Get paid early at your buyer’s cost of money.

A supplier holding an invoice its buyer has approved can be paid today by the cheapest source of liquidity: the buyer’s own cash, or a bank pricing off the buyer’s credit. OpenFloat is the open, operator-less protocol that makes this possible, with an Apache-2.0 reference implementation.

The problem

The same invoice is priced twice, depending on whose credit you look at.

Small suppliers who need cash before an invoice is due usually turn to factoring, priced off their own credit. Once a large buyer has confirmed it will pay, that invoice is close to a buyer-quality asset. The spread between the two is captured today inside closed, single-operator platforms.

Closed silos are opaque

Each supply-chain-finance programme lives on one operator’s platform. Funders on one platform cannot see what was financed on another, and none of them is in a position to see everything a supplier has pledged.

Double financing keeps recurring

The same receivable funded more than once is a recurring failure mode. It is under investigation in the 2025 First Brands bankruptcy, where receivables were allegedly financed more than once (allegations only)6,7. In the 2021 Greensill collapse, about $10bn of supply-chain-finance funds were frozen when the platform's insurance cover lapsed8.

How it works

Signed messages between the parties. No platform in the middle.

Every participant runs its own node. Nodes exchange Ed25519-signed messages directly and resolve each other’s keys through a participant directory. Claims that rely on another party’s word carry that party’s signed message as embedded proof, so any node can verify without trusting anyone in between.

1

The buyer signs a promise

When the buyer approves an invoice, its node signs a payment undertaking: pay this amount on this date. That signed promise is what lets funders price off the buyer’s credit rather than the supplier’s.

2

The cheapest money wins

The supplier asks for early payment. Funders reply with firm offers; the buyer can offer to pay early from its own cash, deciding with a cash-floor-aware optimiser. The supplier accepts one.

3

The buyer acknowledges once

If a funder wins, the buyer’s node records it as the new payee and acknowledges once. The funder pays only after that acknowledgment, and the buyer pays the funder at maturity.

Double financing

Why no one can finance an invoice twice.

Three independent checks, each covering a different party who might try.

1

The buyer acknowledges once

The buyer owes the money, so its node decides who gets paid at maturity, the same logic as a notice of assignment in receivables law. The first valid assignment moves the invoice to ASSIGNED; every later one is rejected. Funders fund only after a matching acknowledgment.

2

The supplier signs a representation

Every ACCEPT carries the supplier’s signed statement that the invoice is not assigned or pledged. The protocol cannot see a factor or lender outside it; this does not prevent that, but it turns each acceptance into signed evidence that gives the funder recourse.

3

Acknowledgments are published

A dishonest buyer could acknowledge the same invoice to two funders, each seeing only its own. Funders publish every acknowledgment to an append-only, hash-chained log, in the style of Certificate Transparency, then check it. A later funder finds the earlier entry and does not pay out.

What the log holds

Each entry has a sequence number, the invoice hash, the buyer and funder ids, a digest of the signed acknowledgment, the previous entry’s hash, and the funder’s signature. No amounts or prices. Anyone can recompute the chain; altering, inserting, removing or reordering an entry is detected.

Limitation: a log operator cannot forge or rewrite entries, but could withhold them. The production answer, as in Certificate Transparency, is several independent logs, mirrors and signed tree heads exchanged by gossip. The reference implementation runs a single in-process log.

Every message is also signed over canonical JSON, recursively through embedded proofs, so tampering in transit, forged buyer approvals, and replayed notices are rejected.

seq 41inv 9f2c…a1 · bank-ano earlier entry: funds
↓ prev_hash links each entry to the one before
seq 42inv 07be…5d · bank-bfunds
↓ …
seq 57inv 9f2c…a1 · bank-bearlier entry, seq 41: does not fund

Illustrative entries. A funder publishes first, then checks, so of two funders racing on one invoice the later always sees the earlier.

Settlement

Money moves on rails you already have.

Nodes never move money. They emit settlement instructions to the banks participants already use, then exchange signed confirmations of what those banks report. Each node speaks only for its own bank.

InstructionThe payer’s node emits a signed settlement instruction
→
Payment fileRendered as an ISO 20022 pain.001 file for the payer’s bank
→
Banks executeOrdinary credit transfer between existing accounts
→
Payer confirmsPAYMENT_STATUS from its bank’s pain.002 report
→
Payee confirmsPAYMENT_RECEIVED from its bank’s camt.054 credit

One reference end to end

The instruction’s message id travels as the pain.001 EndToEndId through the bank status report and the credit notification, so every bank movement maps to exactly one protocol instruction.

Missing payments are evidenced

Every payee tracks what it expects. If no matching credit arrives by the due date plus a grace period, its node sends a signed PAYMENT_OVERDUE. Together with the buyer’s undertaking or acknowledgment, that supports enforcement under the applicable law.

The protocol coordinates and evidences obligations. Banks move money. The law enforces obligations.

Deliberately out of scope

  • Mechanisms that compel payment, such as direct-debit mandates, a bank acting as paying agent, or programmable money and atomic settlement. They depend on jurisdiction and licensing, so they are left to bilateral arrangements between participants and their banks.
  • Card issuers as funders. A card issuer lends to the buyer, and a card payment extinguishes the invoice rather than assigning it. A future version may model card as a buyer-side payment option.

Where things stand

  • The reference implementation writes well-formed pain.001.001.09 files; they are not yet validated against the official XSD.
  • Account and BIC data are synthetic placeholders. Mock bank rails stand in for participants’ banks in the simulation. No real money moves.

Protocol, not platform

No central operator to trust, fund or outlive.

For each component, the design asks one question: is there a party everyone would trust to run this? For approved payables, there is: the obligor. Only the buyer can say who it will pay at maturity, so the buyer’s node is the registry. No shared ledger of invoices is needed.

Consortium networks shut down

we.trade closed in 2022, Marco Polo became insolvent in 2023, and Contour closed in 2023. Each shut when its bank owners stopped funding it9. A protocol with no operator has no single owner whose withdrawal ends it.

Open networks already work

Peppol e-invoicing connects independent access points through a shared specification and directory. Singapore’s InvoiceNow, built on Peppol, is being extended to all GST-registered businesses, phased from 2028 to 203110. OpenFloat aims to map invoices to Peppol BIS Billing 3.0.

Governance: the intent

The intended home for the specification and participant directory is a neutral foundation (FINOS-style) with change control and conformance testing, and no single commercial owner. GLEIF vLEI is the leading candidate for binding node keys to legal entities. Both are open questions, not settled.

For each participant

What each party gets.

Buyers

  • Earn a discount by paying early from surplus cash, decided by a policy that respects your daily cash floor and payables forecast.
  • Stronger suppliers, without running or paying for a platform.
  • Stay the source of truth: you acknowledge at most one payee per invoice and pay that payee at maturity.

Suppliers

  • Get paid on an approved invoice at a price based on your buyer’s credit, not your own.
  • Competing firm offers from the buyer and any participating funder; take the cheapest.
  • Signed confirmations of every payment, and overdue notices if one is late.

Banks and funders

  • Buyer-quality assets, backed by the buyer’s signed undertaking and acknowledgment.
  • Built-in double-financing protection: fund only after the acknowledgment and a clean log check, with the supplier’s signed representation as recourse.
  • Settlement through your existing payment systems, as standard pain.001 files.

See it run

A reference implementation you can run locally.

The dashboard walks through one buyer, two banks and two suppliers exchanging real signed messages, step by step. One supplier tries to finance the same invoice twice and a message is tampered with in transit; both are rejected.

Quickstart (Python 3.12+ and uv)

git clone https://github.com/4rnaudD/openfloat && cd openfloat
uv sync
uv run openfloat dashboard   # walkthrough, network outcome, message log
uv run openfloat simulate    # synthetic network: 1 buyer, 25 suppliers, 2 banks
uv run pytest                # protocol and invariant tests

Where it fails

The buyer’s payables forecast is a simple run-rate. When actual invoices exceed it, early payments can still push the balance under the floor for a few days. A better forecast, or a safety margin on the floor, would fix that.

Roadmap and open questions

A draft, published to be challenged.

Next

  • HTTPS transport and directory lookup, so nodes run independently
  • Plain-language offer explanations for suppliers (LLM-assisted, never deciding)
  • Dilution (credit notes, disputes), partial financing, term-extension guardrails
  • Mapping invoices to Peppol BIS Billing 3.0 / InvoiceNow
  • Card payments as a buyer-side payment option (pay by card now, settle on the card statement), not a funder
  • Not yet in 0.1: key rotation and revocation; several independent acknowledgment logs with signed tree heads and gossip

Open questions in the spec

  • Governance. Who owns the specification and the participant directory? Intended: a neutral foundation with change control and conformance testing.
  • Identity. Binding node keys to verified legal entities; GLEIF vLEI is the leading candidate.
  • Sustainability. Maintenance funded by hosted nodes, compliance tooling and certification, without a fee on every transaction.
  • Dilution. A defined rule for how credit notes and disputes affect assigned invoices.
